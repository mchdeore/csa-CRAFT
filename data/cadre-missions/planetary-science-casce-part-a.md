# Introduction

## Purpose of this document

CADRE Part A provides a concise, standardized synopsis of the mission or
project to support costing and analysis activities. Its primary purpose
is to consolidate information from multiple technical and programmatic
source documents into a single, normalized reference.

Specifically, CADRE Part A:

- Summarizes key mission characteristics in a consistent and structured
  format

- Aggregates information drawn from multiple authoritative technical
  documents

- Reduces the need for analysts to repeatedly consult disparate source
  materials

- Ensures a common baseline description is used across analyses

- Improves efficiency, traceability, and consistency in downstream
  costing work

In practical terms, CADRE Part A functions as a time-saving reference
document that enables analysts to quickly understand the mission context
without re-interpreting or reconciling multiple source documents.

# General Descriptive Information

## Mission Overview

The \*\*Canadian Asteroid Surface and Composition Explorer (CASCE)\*\*
is a proposed Canadian scientific instrument that would fly as part of a
European deep-space planetary mission. The mission's primary objective
is to identify and characterize small asteroids that have not yet been
studied in detail, with particular emphasis on understanding their
composition, surface properties, and thermal behaviour.

CASCE would operate as a remote-sensing instrument, observing selected
asteroids during close encounters and collecting measurements across
multiple wavelengths. These observations would allow scientists to
distinguish between different surface materials and investigate how
asteroids have evolved since the early Solar System. The instrument
would also contribute measurements of asteroid size, temperature,
surface variability, and potential indicators of hydrated or carbon-rich
material.

The Canadian contribution would consist of the CASCE instrument and its
associated data-processing system, while the spacecraft, launch vehicle,
and primary mission infrastructure would be provided through a
European-led partnership. This arrangement would give Canada a focused
scientific role within a larger international planetary mission while
allowing the instrument team to develop specialized capabilities in
asteroid observation and analysis.

A key feature of CASCE would be its ability to operate autonomously
during short observation opportunities. Because spacecraft resources and
asteroid encounters are constrained, the instrument would prioritize
observations based on target visibility, expected scientific value,
spacecraft pointing constraints, and available communications and power.
The resulting data would be transmitted to Earth for calibration,
processing, and scientific analysis.

The mission would ultimately create a comparative dataset of asteroid
properties, helping researchers investigate the diversity of small
bodies in the Solar System and improve understanding of the materials
from which planets and other larger bodies formed. The fictional mission
therefore combines Canadian instrumentation expertise with European
spacecraft capabilities to conduct a focused investigation of previously
poorly characterized asteroids.

## Context 

The conceptual mission draws organizational and programmatic inspiration
from **STONECHASER**, Canada\'s deep-space instrument contribution
developed for a prior international planetary mission. STONECHASER
demonstrates relevant Canadian heritage in international instrument
contribution, deep-space remote sensing, and long-duration
government-industry mission delivery.

STONECHASER demonstrates relevant Canadian heritage in:

- international instrument contribution to a foreign-led deep-space
  mission;

- small-body and planetary remote sensing;

- government-industry cooperation between CSA and Canadian industry;

- small, focused instrument team management within a larger mission
  structure;

- science data delivery and archiving agreements with an international
  partner; and

- long-duration deep-space instrument operations.

STONECHASER\'s instrument design, host mission, and target body were
substantially different from the proposed CASCE mission. It is therefore
used only as a programmatic heritage reference; the CASCE cost and
schedule baseline is driven by the CASCE architecture, current PM
schedule, procurement model, and finalized CADRe Part C / Part D
assumptions.

Reference industry responses and market observations are retained only
as concept-development evidence; they do not establish CASCE procurement
partners. The finalized procurement baseline is defined in CADRe Part D
and identifies CSA, ESA, Northstar Space Systems, Borealis Optical
Systems, Nordholt Imaging Technologies, Chinook Avionics, and Canadian
university/research institutions as the relevant organizations.

The CASCE CADRe baseline in this document is the controlling Part A
reference for the current cost estimate. Any change to the mission
architecture, schedule, procurement model, or organizational baseline
shall be reconciled with the finalized CADRe Part C and Part D
documents.

## High-Level Mission Architecture 

Owned and operated by CSA, the mission consists of

- A **Government Segment**: consisting of government resources for
  oversight and their associated management costs such as T&L

- A **Space Segment**: consisting of four spacecrafts with a
  platform/bus and a infrared instrument.

- A **Launch Segment**: consisting of the launcher and launch service
  provider. Provided by ESA.

- A **Ground Segment**: consisting of facilities and equipment to plan
  and command acquisitions, as well as, to receive, archive and process
  data acquired by the satellite. Provided by ESA.

- An **Ops Segment**: consisting of personnel to handle real-time
  operations and some O&M costs. Provided by ESA.

- A **Science Segment**: consisting of scientific leadership, research
  teams, and supporting infrastructure responsible for mission science
  planning, calibration and validation, data analysis, scientific data
  products, user support, and the maximization of scientific return from
  the mission.

# Space Segment Description

The space segment consists of a constellation of 4 spacecrafts, each
with a bus and a payload. The 4^th^ spacecraft is an on-orbit spare.

## Spacecraft Bus 

CASCE would be accommodated on a mid-size interplanetary spacecraft bus
supplied by the European mission partner. The bus is a modular,
flight-proven platform with prior heritage in deep-space orbiter and
flyby missions, built around a composite honeycomb structure sized to
support multiple science instruments through extended cruise phases and
discrete close-approach encounters.

Power comes from deployable solar arrays feeding a rechargeable battery,
sized for the mission\'s operating distance from the Sun. Propulsion
combines a main chemical engine for trajectory maneuvers with smaller
thrusters for the fine pointing needed during asteroid encounters,
supported by star trackers, reaction wheels, and gyroscopes for attitude
stability. Thermal control uses multi-layer insulation, radiators, and
heaters, with a dedicated interface protecting CASCE\'s
temperature-sensitive infrared detector.

The bus provides a shared communications subsystem --- a high-gain
antenna for science downlink and a low-gain antenna for housekeeping ---
along with onboard data storage to buffer science data between
encounters and scheduled contacts. Its command and data handling system
supports autonomous operation during long communication delays,
coordinating instrument pointing and data collection within trajectory,
power, and thermal constraints. CASCE interfaces with this system
through a standard instrument data and command bus, drawing power and
pointing support from the host while running its own internal
electronics.

This reflects a class of mission-proven interplanetary platform rather
than a specific existing design, consistent with the partner-provided
spacecraft arrangement in the mission concept.

## CASCE Payload

The CASCE instrument is a compact multi-wavelength remote-sensing
package, developed under Canadian industry leadership with subsystems
contributed by a mix of Canadian companies. Northstar Space Systems
(Toronto, ON) acts as instrument prime, integrating the subsystems below
and managing the interface to the European-provided spacecraft bus.

### Optics & telescope assembly 

Shared optical path feeding visible, near-infrared, and thermal infrared
detectors, in a folded reflective design that minimizes instrument
volume. Lead: Borealis Optical Systems (Ottawa, ON)

### Detector suite 

Visible/NIR imaging array paired with an uncooled or lightly cooled
thermal infrared microbolometer array for surface temperature mapping.
Lead: Nordholt Imaging Technologies (Waterloo, ON)

### Instrument electronics & data processing 

Onboard processor handling detector readout, data compression,
autonomous target-prioritization logic, and instrument health
monitoring. Lead: Chinook Avionics (Calgary, AB)

### Thermal control 

Passive radiator and heater system maintaining detector and electronics
temperatures within operating limits through cruise and encounters.
Lead: Laurentide Thermal Solutions (Montréal, QC)

### Structure & mechanisms 

Instrument housing, spacecraft mounting interface, and any scan or
pointing mechanism supporting target tracking. Lead: Precambrian
Structures Inc. (Sudbury, ON)

### Calibration system 

Onboard calibration source and reference targets supporting radiometric
accuracy across all spectral bands. Lead: Meridian Calibration Labs
(Halifax, NS)

### Systems integration & test 

Overall instrument integration, environmental qualification, and
interface verification with the spacecraft bus. Lead: Northstar Space
Systems (Toronto, ON)

# Launch Segment description

The CASCE-hosting observatory will be launched aboard the Aurora-VI
heavy-lift launch vehicle, injecting the spacecraft onto a direct
transfer trajectory toward the Sun-Earth L2 Lagrange point. Following a
transit period of approximately 30 days, the observatory will execute a
series of trajectory correction maneuvers culminating in insertion into
a halo orbit about L2, selected for its stable thermal environment,
unobstructed sky visibility, and favorable conditions for sustained
asteroid observation campaigns.

The launch vehicle is responsible for delivering the observatory to its
target transfer trajectory within the accuracy required to minimize
propellant expenditure for subsequent station-keeping and
orbit-insertion maneuvers. Launch would occur from a European spaceport,
consistent with the mission\'s European-led spacecraft and launch
provision described in the mission concept. ESA is providing launch.

# Ground Segment and Mission Operations

Mission operations for the observatory, including spacecraft commanding,
orbit determination, station-keeping, and health and safety monitoring,
will be conducted by ESA through its established ground segment and
mission operations centre. In this arrangement, ESA also commands and
controls the CASCE instrument itself, since CASCE is a passive optical
instrument with no active pointing or scan mechanisms of its own ---
instrument operation is achieved through spacecraft pointing and
standard command sequences integrated into ESA\'s overall mission
timeline. This significantly reduces the scope of Canadian ground
infrastructure required to support the mission, as no dedicated
instrument command and control centre is needed.

Science and housekeeping data collected by CASCE will be downlinked via
the ESA ground station network to ESA\'s mission operations centre, then
delivered to an ESA-operated Science Data Centre responsible for
archiving all observational data from the mission, including calibration
products and metadata. This centre will serve as the authoritative
long-term archive for the mission\'s science data, consistent with
standard practice for ESA-led planetary science missions.

Canadian access to CASCE data will be provided through the Canadian
Astronomy Data Centre (CADC), operated by the National Research Council
of Canada, which will retrieve CASCE data products from the ESA Science
Data Centre under a data-sharing agreement between the Canadian Space
Agency and ESA. Canadian scientists will access, search, and download
CASCE observations through the CADC\'s existing data access tools and
user interfaces, consistent with how Canadian researchers access data
from other international astronomy missions. This approach avoids
duplicating ESA\'s data processing and archiving infrastructure while
ensuring Canadian researchers have timely, familiar access to the
mission\'s science products.

The interface between ESA\'s Science Data Centre and the CADC will be
governed by a data-sharing agreement specifying data formats, delivery
cadence, product versioning, and points of contact for resolving
data-quality or delivery issues.

# System Engineering and project management approach

## TRL Levels and Heritage

CASCE will draw on heritage evidence where applicable, but the finalized
baseline does not assume inheritance of the TRL of any reference
spacecraft or instrument. Major spacecraft bus components are targeted
to reach TRL ≥ 8 prior to flight qualification. Critical payload
technologies are expected to be at TRL ≥ 6 at the start of Phase B2
development, advancing to TRL ≥ 8 prior to flight qualification.
Commercial or heritage qualification evidence may only be reused where
the configuration, environment, interfaces, and operating assumptions
are demonstrated to be applicable to CASCE.

## Phasing Logic and Naming conventions

For the finalized CADRe cost baseline, the PM nomenclature is B1, B2, C,
D, E, and F. The nominal schedule is: B1 = 13 months (September
2026--October 2027); B2 = 9 months (October 2027--July 2028); C = 9
months (July 2028--April 2029); D = 25 months (April 2029--May 2031,
including manufacturing, integration, test, launch and commissioning
activities); E = 60 months (May 2031--May 2036); F = approximately 2
months for disposal/close-out. Thus, the nominal PM schedule contains
116 months from B1 start through the end of nominal operations, followed
by a separate approximately 2-month Phase F close-out period. The
detailed optimistic and pessimistic scenarios remain controlled in CADRe
Part C and are not duplicated here.

## Safety and Mission Assurance

The CASCE program requires formal mission assurance, qualification,
acceptance, verification, configuration control, and readiness reviews.
Safety and mission-assurance effort is a direct cost and schedule
driver, as the CASCE flight instrument must independently demonstrate
conformity with the qualified design before delivery to the European
spacecraft prime for integration and launch.

## Qualification and Acceptance Strategy, MODEL Philosophy

The CASCE qualification and acceptance strategy will verify that the
instrument is capable of surviving launch and performing its required
functions throughout the planned mission lifetime. The approach uses one
Qualification Model (QM) followed by a single Flight Model (FM), with
engineering-model hardware used only where necessary to verify
interfaces and reduce development risk.

The Qualification Model will represent the flight configuration as
closely as practical and will undergo instrument-level environmental and
functional testing, including vibration, thermal-vacuum, electromagnetic
compatibility, and end-to-end functional testing. Optical performance,
detector operation, electronics, data handling, and the interface to the
host spacecraft will be verified. Existing qualification evidence may be
used only where applicable to the CASCE configuration and operating
environment.

Following successful qualification, the Flight Model will be
manufactured to the qualified design and will undergo its own
functional, electrical, calibration, and appropriate environmental
acceptance testing before delivery to the European spacecraft prime for
integration

The program also accounts for delivery logistics: the instrument will be
transported under controlled configuration and environmental conditions,
supported through integration with the host spacecraft, pre-launch
functional checks, and secure transfer of configuration data.

Following launch, the instrument will undergo a commissioning period
targeted to complete within 90 days, verifying power, pointing support,
detector performance, calibration, initial observation quality, and
ground-segment interfaces before transitioning to nominal operations.

All qualification and acceptance results will be documented through the
CASCE verification and configuration-management process. Any anomalies
identified during testing, delivery, integration, or commissioning will
be assessed and resolved before CASCE is declared operational. Final
approval for flight and entry into operational service will be based on
completion of required verification activities and successful Flight
Readiness and Operations Readiness Reviews.

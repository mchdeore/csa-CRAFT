# Building the CSA’s Artificial Intelligence Capability
A Business Case for a Secure, Shared AI Experimentation Environment
Executive Summary:
Artificial intelligence could significantly improve scientific, engineering and
corporate work at the CSA. However, the Agency currently lacks a secure and
accessible environment in which to evaluate these capabilities. This horizontal
initiative proposes testing CRAFT through SSC’s GC Cloud One: LaunchPad using
controlled use cases and measurable performance indicators. The results will
support a decision to discontinue, refine or advance each capability toward an
operational pilot.
AI Collective, 30 July 2026

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. The Transformative Potential of Artificial Intelligence
Artificial intelligence has the potential to improve how information is collected, structured,
analyzed and communicated. It can process large volumes of structured and unstructured
information, identify patterns and inconsistencies, automate repetitive activities, and
make specialized knowledge more accessible to employees.
When combined with authoritative documents, databases and appropriate business rules,
AI can support scientists, engineers and analysts throughout the information lifecycle.
Potential applications include extracting information from technical documents, organizing
data in structured repositories, searching across multiple information sources, generating
analyses and visualizations, identifying anomalies, and preparing draft reports and
recommendations.
AI can also improve decision support by helping users bring together information that is
currently dispersed across documents, spreadsheets, databases and corporate systems.
This could reduce the time spent locating and preparing information, allowing specialists
to focus more attention on interpretation, validation and higher-value work.
The objective is not to replace scientific, engineering or financial judgment. Rather, AI can
augment professional expertise by accelerating routine tasks, highlighting issues that
warrant attention and presenting relevant evidence to support human decisions. To realize
these benefits responsibly, the CSA must develop the capacity to evaluate AI technologies,
understand their limitations and determine where they can provide measurable operational
value.
1.2. Problem Statement
The CSA currently lacks an approved, secure and accessible experimentation environment
where scientists, engineers and analysts can develop and test new technologies, data
solutions and innovative digital capabilities. Access to essential tools -including artificial
intelligence, cloud computing, software-development platforms, data-processing services,
application hosting and SQL databases-is limited, fragmented or unavailable tousers.
Existing corporate environments are largely siloed, with information distributed across
disconnected systems, documents and spreadsheets, and without a common data model
or authoritative single source of truth.
Existing corporate environments are primarily designed to support stable operational
systems. They are not necessarily configured to support rapid experimentation, iterative
development or the temporary deployment of proofs of concept. Obtaining access to new
tools and services can involve lengthy processes, while locally developed solutions may
not have an approved pathway for testing, integration or eventual transition to production.
These constraints make it difficult to explore emerging technologies, integrate and analyze
complex datasets, develop prototypes, conduct proofs of concept, and evaluate potential
solutions before committing to operational implementation. They also limit collaboration

The absence of an experimentation environment also restricts the CSA’s ability to build

cannot easily assess the accuracy, limitations, security implications, costs or operational
value of emerging technologies. This makes it more difficult to define informed
requirements, challenge vendor claims, select appropriate solutions and oversee
externally delivered work.
As AI and cloud technologies are adopted more broadly across government, the CSA risks
falling behind organizations that already provide their employees with controlled
opportunities to experiment and learn. The challenge is therefore broader than access to
individual tools: the Agency would benefit froman enabling environment in which
innovation can be explored safely, evidence can be collected, and promising concepts can
be evaluated before investments or operational commitments are made.
1.3. Merits of Controlled Experimentation
Controlled experimentation provides a low-risk, evidence-based way to explore emerging
technologies and determine whether they can solve real business problems. Instead of
committing immediately to a large implementation, the CSA can begin with bounded use
cases, synthetic or approved data, limited system access and clearly defined success
criteria.
An experimentation approach allows the Agency to test both the technology and the
conditions required for its successful adoption. It can help determine:
- whether the proposed capability is technically feasible;
- whether it produces accurate, reliable and traceable results;
- whether it can be integrated with existing documents, databases and workflows;
- what security, privacy and information-management controls are required;
- whether users find the capability useful and trustworthy;
- what skills, governance and operational support would be needed; and
- whether the expected benefits justify the implementation and operating costs.
Experimentation also provides value when a concept does not succeed. Identifying
technical limitations, poor-quality data, unsuitable use cases or excessive costs at an early
stage prevents larger investments in solutions that may not meet the Agency’s needs. The
result is a more informed basis for deciding whether to discontinue, refine, scale or
operationalize a capability.
1.4. About GC Cloud One
Shared Services Canada (SSC) recently introduced GC Cloud One: LaunchPad as part of
its initiative to progressively centralize cloud services across the Government of Canada.
LaunchPad was created, in part, to address the need for controlled cloud environments
where federal scientists and engineers can conduct research, experimentation and proofs
of concept.

2. PROPOSED EXPERIMENT
A horizontal initiative is proposed across CSA branches and directorates to develop and
evaluateCRAFT -Collaborative Reasoning and Federated Technical Knowledge-as a
shared AI experimentation platform.
CRAFT would support the controlled exploration of AI applications across scientific,
engineering, operational and corporate activities. Potential use cases include document
and knowledge management, risk assessment, cost estimation, anomaly detection,
mission-planning support, data analysis, and report generation.
2.1. Experimentation objectives
The experiment will evaluate a progressive set of AI-enabled capabilities, beginning with
low-risk, controlled use cases. These may include extracting information from test
documents, proposing values for structured SQL fields, answering questions using
documents and database records, generating tables and charts, drafting analytical
observations, guiding users through the costing process, and supporting data-quality
reviews and imputation.
The initial experiment will use synthetic, anonymized or otherwise approved information.
The experiment is intended to answer three fundamental questions:
1. Can the AI produce sufficiently accurate, consistent and traceable results?
2. Can it be integrated safely with documents, SQL databases and analytical tools?
3. Does it create measurable value compared with the current way of working?
The results will inform the organization’s AI Capability and Assurance Maturity Model and
help determine which capabilities are suitable for further development, operational
deployment or discontinuation.
The experiment will conclude with a final report documenting the findings, lessons learned,
performance against the established success criteria, and recommendations on whether
each capability should be discontinued, further developed or advanced to a controlled
operational pilot.
2.2. Multidisciplinary approach towards a common solution
The experiment would be conducted as a horizontal initiative involving several teams
across the CSA, with each participating area contributing its expertise and evaluating use
cases relevant to its mandate:

3. MEASURING SUCCESS
Maturity models provide a structured method for assessing current capabilities and
defining a progressive path toward more advanced, reliable and controlled practices. The
AI Capability and Assurance Maturity Model will be used to evaluate each use case
consistently and determine whether it is ready to progress from experimentation to
controlled operational deployment.
In addition to the maturity model, each use case will be evaluated using measurable key
performance indicators. These indicators may include response time, extraction and
classification accuracy, error and hallucination rates, the accuracy of information retrieved
and federated across multiple sources, source traceability, consistency of repeated
results, human correction rates, system availability, user satisfaction, time saved and
operating cost.
Performance thresholds will reflect the risk and intended use of each capability. Higher
levels of accuracy, traceability and human validation will be required for database
transactions, financial analysis and operational decision support than for lower-risk
activities such as summarization or drafting.

4. PROGRAM TERMS AND CONDITIONS
4.1. Budget
The total expected budget for the experiment is 100K covering 12 calendar months.
4.2. Procurement strategy
Services would be prepaid within a Gov’t of Canada Cloud Framework Agreement. Funds
would be transferred via a journal entry.
As services are consumed, the associated amount is deducted against the prepaid amount
on a monthly basis. Shared Services would charge a 10% brokering fee as coordinator of
the services. Training Advisory and Support services are also available.
Once the experimentation is completed, if the CSA decides to expand or continue into
operations, then a cost estimate would be required.
The procurement strategy changes depending on the value of the project:
- GC organizations can use LaunchPad for a single experiment valued up to $500,000
(+taxes).
- If the total project cost is between 500K and 4.5M, Shared Services will issue a
standing offer
- If the project total is higher than 4.5M, it must be competed.
4.3. Schedule and Life-Cycle
The expected duration of experimentation is 12 months.

4.4. Security Considerations
4.4.1.1. Security classification
Although the services is ratedProtected B, the experiment will begin using unclassified
data. Fake (dummy) data will be produced for testing purposes. As the proof-of-concept
advances, it may be necessary to add protected B data. Where possible, we would
anonymize, modify and obfuscate the data to protect sensitive info.
For example: if the focal plane array resolution is protected B, we could replace the number
with a different one (a fake one).
4.4.1.2. Connectivity
The cloud environment remains separate from the CSA internal network. There is no
persistent network connection between the cloud environment and GC systems.
Authorized users or GC system may send an approved copy of data from inside the GC
network to the cloud environment through an approved transfer mechanism.
In practical terms, the experimentation environment will permit:
- Export approved data from SAP, SQL, Excel or a document repository and upload or
push the copy to LaunchPad.
- Periodically send an approved dataset through an authorized HTTPS endpoint, API
or file-transfer service, if permitted by the applicable security rules.
If the experimentation eventually leads to a production environment, at a different service
level, then we can examine the pros and cons of:
- LettingLaunchPad connect directly into the CSA network to query SAP, Oracle,
shared drives or internal repositories.
- Establishinga VPN or SCED connection that makes LaunchPad part of, or privately
connected to, the GC network.
4.4.1.3. Human Control and Accountability
CRAFT is designed toassist people, not replace them. Human authority remains central,
especially forimportant decisions.

# Appendix A-Mapping Costing Use Cases
A.1. Intelligent Document Intake
AI reads documents and:
- identifies the document type;
- extracts candidate values;
- maps them to SQL tables and fields;
- shows the source passage;
- assigns a confidence level;
- highlights ambiguous or missing information;
- presents the proposed values to a person;
- performs the POST only after approval.
Capability Level 3 because it demonstrates useful automation without allowing
uncontrolled database changes.
A.2. Conversational knowledge retrieval
AI combines information from:
- policies, standards and project documents;
- historical estimates;
- the costing semantic model;
- SAP or financial extracts;
- schedules and engineering parameters.
It answers in plain language while identifying the source, reporting date and applicable
baseline. This progresses from Capability Level 2 retrieval to Level 4 when it can safely
execute approved queries.
A.3. Conversational analytics
A user could ask: Show annual expenditures by mission phase and compare actual costs
against the approved baseline.
The AI would:
1. interpret the request;
2. generate the SQL;
3. validate the fields and filters;
4. execute the query;
5. populate a table or chart;
6. describe assumptions and limitations.
A.4. Analytical interpretation and reporting
AI interprets results and drafts:

- trends and anomalies;

AI could:
- detect inconsistent SAP coding;
- identify duplicate or incorrectly classified transactions;
- find abnormal labour or procurement costs;
- reconcile SAP data with project records;
- detect missing engineering or cost parameters;
- recommend comparable projects;
- propose imputed values and ranges;
- explain the imputation method;
- preserve the original value and record the calculation pedigree.
Imputed values should always be visibly distinguished from observed data.

# Appendix B-Architecture
CRAFT consists of four main architectural layers:
1. User Access Layer — Enables authorized users to ask questions, submit tasks and
workflows, provide information, and review or approve outputs. This layer includes
web applications and interfaces for search, data ingestion, visualization, reporting
and administration.
2. Reasoning and Agent Layer —Interprets requests, breaks complex tasks into
manageable steps, retrieves relevant evidence, coordinates specialized AI agents,
prepares responses and assesses confidence. It may also identify missing
information and refer uncertain results for human review.
3. Knowledge Management Layer —Connects approved information sources and
transforms dispersed information into structured, searchable and reusable
organizational knowledge. This layer includes storage and retrieval services for both
structured data, such as SQL databases, and unstructured information, such as
documents and reports.
4. Governance and Integration Layer —Manages security, permissions, system
connections and data movement. It applies applicable business rules, supports
human review and approval, and records sources, actions and decisions to provide
traceability and accountability.
Together, these layers enable CRAFT to help users locate trusted information, reason from
evidence, reuse organizational knowledge and support informed decisions while
maintaining appropriate security, traceability and human control.
